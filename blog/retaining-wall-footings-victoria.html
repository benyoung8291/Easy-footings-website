Retaining Wall Footings in Victoria: Depth, Permits & Costs

By Easy Footings • 19 July 2026 • 11 min read

Short answer: a retaining wall footing has a different job to the footings under a house, deck or carport. Instead of resisting the weight of a structure or wind trying to lift it off the ground, it has to resist lateral earth pressure — the sideways push of the retained soil trying to tip and slide the wall forward. In Victoria, any wall 1 metre or higher needs a building permit and an engineer, the footing (or post) design is governed by AS 4678 Earth Retaining Structures, and getting the drainage behind the wall right matters almost as much as the footing itself.

This guide covers when you actually need a permit, how deep retaining wall footings and posts need to be, the difference between a gravity wall, a sleeper wall on posts and a screw-pile-supported wall, why drainage is not optional, and what retaining walls cost across Victoria in 2026. If your retaining wall is really a sloping-block problem for a house or extension, see our guide to footings on sloping blocks; for the soil science behind all of this, see our guide to concrete footings and AS 2870 soil classes.

Why a Retaining Wall Is a Different Footing Problem

Most of the structures we cover in this blog — carports, pergolas, sheds, decks — are light and open to the wind, so their footings are designed against uplift: the wind trying to pull the posts up and out of the ground. A retaining wall flips that problem on its head. There is no wind uplift to speak of, but there is a permanent, constant sideways force from the soil behind the wall, pushing to tip it forward and slide it out at the base. Add water that isn't drained away and that lateral pressure can roughly double.

That is why retaining wall footings and posts are sized for overturning and sliding resistance rather than tension. A gravity wall resists it with its own mass. A sleeper wall on steel posts resists it by embedding each post deep enough, and with enough soil bearing in front of the embedded section, that the post cannot rotate forward. A screw pile resists it the same way a house stump resists uplift — by engaging a much larger volume of soil than the pile itself, this time loaded sideways rather than in tension.

Do You Need a Permit for a Retaining Wall in Victoria?

The Victorian Building Authority's rule of thumb is straightforward, but it has real teeth in practice:

  • A retaining wall under 1 metre in height is generally exempt from a building permit — unless it is associated with other building work, or it is protecting adjoining property.
  • A retaining wall 1 metre or higher always needs a building permit, and the design must be certified by a structural engineer.
  • Protecting adjoining property is the trap many homeowners miss: if your wall holds back soil that would otherwise slump onto a neighbour's land, the exemption falls away regardless of height, because you are protecting their property, not just yours.
  • Several Melbourne councils, including Boroondara, Bayside and Stonnington, apply a stricter local threshold and can require a permit for boundary retaining walls as low as 600 mm.
  • A wall that supports a surcharge — a driveway, shed, pool or anything else built on the high side — needs to be engineered for that additional load regardless of its own height.

For the wider picture on how footing designs get approved in Victoria, see our guide to building footing regulations in Victoria. In practice: check with your building surveyor or council before you dig, even for a wall that looks like it should be exempt.

How Deep Do Retaining Wall Footings Need to Be?

As with every footing on this site, there is no single legal depth — AS 4678 requires the footing or post to be engineered for the retained height, the soil's earth-pressure coefficient, drainage condition and any surcharge. But there are reliable rules of thumb:

  • Sleeper wall steel posts: embedment of roughly one-third of the total post length, plus 100 mm for a drainage gravel bed under the post. A 1.2 m exposed wall on a 1.8 m post typically needs 600 mm or more in the ground, concreted in a bored hole around 450 mm in diameter.
  • Reinforced concrete strip or pad footing (gravity or cantilever wall): commonly 300-600 mm deep and proportioned in width for walls under 1 m; walls over 1 m need a much wider, deeper, reinforced base designed specifically for the site.
  • Screw pile posts (SurePile): typically installed 1.2-2.5 m deep, wound in until the installation torque confirms the lateral and vertical capacity the engineer specified — not measured to a fixed depth on a drawing.

The depth number matters less than what it achieves: enough embedment, in undisturbed and adequately compacted soil, that the post or footing cannot rotate or slide under the retained load. On Melbourne's reactive clay, that also means getting the footing below the seasonally active zone — a shallow, hand-dug post hole that sits entirely inside the active zone will move with the clay's wet-season swell and dry-season shrink, working the post loose over successive years even if it never fails outright in one event.

For most Victorian backyard retaining walls under about 1.5 m — timber or concrete sleepers on steel posts — the post footing is the whole engineering problem. That is exactly the scenario where a concrete-free screw pile post is a straightforward swap for a bored, concreted hole: same post, same wall, no excavation spoil and no cure-time wait before the sleepers go in.

Retaining Walls on Reactive Clay and Sloping Blocks

Retaining walls and reactive clay go hand in hand across Melbourne's growth corridors — Werribee, Melton, Tarneit, Point Cook and similar Class H suburbs are also where cut-and-fill retaining is most common, because the same flat basalt-plain geology that makes the clay reactive also means backyards get benched and stepped. Two things compound here:

  • The retained soil itself swells and shrinks seasonally, changing the lateral pressure on the wall through the year — not just once, at construction.
  • A shallow post footing that sits inside the active zone moves with the clay, independent of the wall's own retaining job, and the two effects add together over time.

Founding the posts below the active zone — whether with a deep bored pier or a screw pile driven on torque rather than a fixed depth — removes that seasonal movement from the equation entirely. See our guide to footings on sloping blocks in Melbourne for how this plays out across a whole cut-and-fill site, and our AS 2870 soil class guide for how Class H and E sites are classified.

Drainage: Get This Wrong and the Footing Doesn't Matter

A retaining wall footing is engineered on the assumption that the soil behind the wall is reasonably well drained. Trap water behind the wall instead, and the lateral pressure on the footing can roughly double — which is why poor drainage, not undersized concrete, is the most common cause of retaining wall failure. The standard Victorian residential specification is:

  • Slotted ag pipe along the base of the wall — 65 mm for walls under 600 mm, 90 mm for 600 mm-1.2 m, and 100 mm for walls over 1.2 m or in high water table areas — wrapped in a geotextile sock to keep fines out.
  • Free-draining aggregate backfill immediately behind the wall, rather than the excavated clay being put straight back.
  • A minimum fall of 1:100 along the ag pipe to a proper stormwater outlet, not a dead end.
  • Weep holes through the wall face itself as a backup outlet if the ag line ever blocks.

None of this is exotic or expensive relative to the wall itself — typically $20-$50 per linear metre — but it is the difference between a wall that performs for decades and one that bulges, leans or fails within a few wet winters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you need a permit for a retaining wall in Victoria?

Under VBA guidance, a retaining wall under 1 metre is generally exempt from a building permit unless it is associated with other building work or is protecting adjoining property. Any wall 1 metre or higher always needs a building permit and, above that height, a structural engineer. Several inner and bayside councils, including Boroondara, Bayside and Stonnington, take a stricter local line and can require a permit for boundary walls as low as 600 mm, so check with your building surveyor or council before you dig.

How deep do retaining wall footings need to be?

There is no single legal depth; AS 4678 requires the footing to be engineered for the retained height, soil type and any surcharge load. As rules of thumb, a sleeper wall's steel posts are typically embedded to at least one-third of the total post length plus 100 mm for drainage gravel, so a 1.2 m wall needs roughly 500-600 mm of post in the ground. A reinforced concrete strip footing for a masonry or block wall is commonly 300-600 mm deep for walls under 1 m, deepening substantially above that. Screw pile posts are typically installed 1.2-2.5 m deep, confirmed by installation torque rather than a fixed dimension, which lets them found below Melbourne's reactive clay active zone.

Can a retaining wall be built without concrete footings?

Yes, for post-and-infill walls such as timber or concrete sleeper walls. Instead of augering a post hole and back-filling it with concrete, a helical screw pile such as SurePile can be wound into the ground to the engineer's required torque and used directly as the retaining post, removing excavation spoil and concrete cure time and founding below the reactive clay zone that shallow post holes sit inside. Full gravity or reinforced concrete walls still rely on a concrete base, but the posts of a sleeper-style wall do not have to.

What does a retaining wall cost in Victoria in 2026?

Indicative supplied-and-installed pricing in 2026: timber sleeper walls run about $200-$450 per linear metre, concrete sleeper walls $250-$600 per linear metre, and stone or block walls $350-$700-plus per linear metre. A straightforward 1 metre concrete sleeper wall typically installs for $300-$600 per linear metre, while taller or engineered walls over 1.5 m run from $800 to $2,000-plus per linear metre once engineering, permits and drainage are included. Engineering fees typically add $600-$1,500+GST and a building permit a further $500-$1,500.

Why does a retaining wall need drainage behind it?

A retaining wall footing is designed for the lateral pressure of dry, well-drained soil. Water trapped behind the wall adds hydrostatic pressure on top of the soil pressure and can roughly double the load the footing has to resist, which is a common cause of retaining wall failure. Standard drainage is a slotted ag pipe (65 mm for walls under 600 mm, 90 mm for 600 mm-1.2 m, 100 mm above 1.2 m) wrapped in geotextile sock, bedded in drainage aggregate, laid to a minimum fall of 1:100 to a proper outlet, with weep holes through the wall face as backup.

Do retaining wall footings need to consider the neighbour's property?

Yes. If a retaining wall is protecting an adjoining property, for example by holding back soil that would otherwise slump onto a neighbour's land, Victorian councils generally require a building permit regardless of the wall's height. Footings on or near a boundary also need to be designed so the excavation and post or pier holes do not undermine the neighbouring footing or fence, and surcharge loads from anything built on the high side must be included in the engineering.

The Bottom Line

A retaining wall footing is fighting sideways soil pressure, not wind or weight, and that changes almost everything about how it should be designed. Anything 1 metre or higher, or protecting a neighbour's land, needs a Victorian building permit and an engineer regardless of what it looks like. Below that, embedment depth, drainage and soil movement do the real work: get the posts below the reactive clay active zone, keep water out from behind the wall, and the wall will still be standing straight in twenty years. For the common backyard case — a timber or concrete sleeper wall on steel posts — a concrete-free screw pile is a direct, faster, mess-free swap for a bored and concreted post hole.
