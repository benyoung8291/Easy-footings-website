Shipping Container Home Footings in Victoria: The Complete Guide

By Easy Footings • 17 April 2026 • 8 min read

Shipping container homes are quietly becoming one of the fastest growing alt-housing trends in Victoria — from weekend cabins on Gippsland bush blocks to permanent two-container dwellings on acreage near Macedon and Daylesford. The container itself is the easy bit: it turns up on a truck. What stops most owner-builders in their tracks is the footing design. Reactive Victorian clay, remote sites, steep slopes and permit requirements make the foundation harder than the structure above it. Here is everything you need to know before you lock in a system.

Why container homes need engineered footings

A shipping container is structurally strongest at its four bottom corners — that is where the cast steel corner castings transfer almost the entire load. The floor cross-members and side rails carry some load, but the moment you support a container along only its long edges (for example, on a row of concrete blocks or railway sleepers) you risk bowing the sub-floor, pulling the door frames out of square and voiding any manufacturer certification.

In Victoria, a container installed as a permanent dwelling is treated the same as any other Class 1a building. That means a building permit, engineered footings compliant with AS 2870, and inspections by a registered building surveyor. A row of unreinforced besser blocks or timber sleepers will not pass. Skip this step and you risk rectification orders, insurance refusal and — more importantly — a container that slowly racks out of square on reactive clay.

How many footings does a container home need?

The short answer: more than most people plan for. A typical engineered layout for a single container dwelling in Victoria looks like this:

  • 20ft (6m) container — minimum four corner footings, plus two mid-span footings under the base rails. Six points total.
  • 40ft (12m) container — four corners plus at least two mid-span footings per side. Six to eight points total, depending on live-load allowances and fit-out weight.
  • Twin-container dwelling (side by side) — each container carries its own footing grid, with additional footings at the central joining rail where internal walls transfer load.
  • Stacked containers — upper containers transfer load through the lower container's corner castings, so lower corner footings are significantly upsized. Always engineer-specified.

Spacing and capacity always come back to the soil report. On H1 or H2 reactive clay across Melbourne's north and west, closer spacing and deeper piles are typical. On rock-shelf sites in the Dandenongs or Macedon Ranges, shallower but higher-capacity footings often work.

Your three real footing options

There are really only three defensible footing systems for a container home in Victoria. Each has a place — the right choice depends on your soil, site access and timeline.

1. Screw pile footings (SurePile)

Screw piles are the most popular concrete-free option under container homes, and for good reason. They are installed by a compact drive head mounted on a mini excavator, reach below the reactive zone in Victorian clays, and are certified to a measured torque that correlates directly to load capacity. For a 40ft container you can typically have all footings in the ground in a single day and crane the container on the next.

Learn more about the system in our screw pile footings Melbourne guide or on the SurePile product page.

2. StumpRite concrete-free stumps

StumpRite is a driven-stump system that suits lighter container builds on stable-to-moderately-reactive soils. It installs fast, needs no curing time and produces a clean engineered pad at each corner casting point. Ideal for single-container weekenders on rural Victorian blocks where you want to minimise site disturbance.

3. Concrete piers and pads

Traditional reinforced concrete piers still work — they are just slow. Excavation, reo cage, formwork, pour, and then up to 28 days of curing before you can land the container. On remote bush blocks the concrete supply alone can be a logistical headache. Concrete is usually only the right answer when the site specifically demands it, such as extreme loads or where an engineered raft is required for a multi-unit build.

Matching footings to Victorian site conditions

  • Macedon Ranges, Daylesford, Woodend — often shallow basalt and clay-over-rock. Screw piles with shorter shafts and higher torque ratings perform excellently; avoid long concrete bored piers that can chase rock.
  • Gippsland and East Gippsland bush blocks — deep reactive soils and occasional fill from historical clearing. Screw piles and SurePile systems handle variable depths without changing the rig.
  • Melbourne's western suburbs and Werribee corridor — Class H1/H2 reactive clay. Concrete-free footings anchored below the active zone will dramatically outperform shallow concrete pads that move with the seasons.
  • Mornington Peninsula sandy sites — screw piles achieve capacity through friction and deeper bearing layers; StumpRite often works well for lighter single-container builds.
  • Yarra Ranges and Dandenongs — steep slopes. Screw piles install vertically regardless of surface gradient, and pile lengths can be varied per footing to avoid benching or retaining works.

Permit and paperwork checklist

  1. Soil test — a geotechnical report classifying the site per AS 2870 (A, S, M, H1, H2, E or P).
  2. Engineering design — footing layout, pile or stump type, depth, embedment torque and bracket details.
  3. Building permit — issued by a registered building surveyor. Sheds and non-habitable outbuildings have different thresholds; a habitable container dwelling does not.
  4. Planning permit (if required) — some Victorian councils require a separate planning permit for relocatable or unconventional dwellings. Check your zoning overlay.
  5. Installation records — torque logs, depth logs and a statement of compliance from the footing installer. These protect you and your insurer for the life of the build.

For a deeper breakdown of the rules, see our Victorian building footing regulations guide.

Common mistakes owner-builders make

  • Buying the container before the soil test — then discovering the site is Class P (problem) and the design has to change.
  • Assuming corner-only support is enough. It is not, except for very short-span transport moves.
  • Using unreinforced besser blocks as a substitute for engineered stumps.
  • Skipping isolation bearers between the footing and the container corner casting, which accelerates steel-on-steel corrosion.
  • Forgetting that a container needs tie-down for wind uplift. Screw piles with integrated brackets solve this elegantly; timber blocks do not.

Realistic timeline for a footing-to-landed container

  • Soil test and design — 1 to 2 weeks.
  • Permit approval — 2 to 6 weeks depending on council.
  • Screw pile or StumpRite install — half a day to one day on site.
  • Container delivery and crane — the day after footings, in most cases.

Compared with concrete, concrete-free footings typically save three to five weeks on the critical path for a container home. On an owner-builder timeline, that is the difference between a spring move-in and a winter move-in.
