Tiny Home Footings in Victoria: Permanent vs Moveable Options

By Easy Footings • 17 April 2026 • 8 min read

Tiny homes in Victoria sit in an awkward grey zone. Are they a caravan? A Class 1a dwelling? A shed with a bed in it? The answer — and therefore the footing requirements — depends almost entirely on whether you want the home to move, and whether you want to live in it permanently. Get the classification right first, and the footings follow naturally. This guide walks through the real-world decisions for a tiny home on a Victorian block, from Gippsland acreage to a Macedon Ranges bush lot.

Tiny home on wheels vs permanent tiny home

The first fork in the road is wheels. A registered tiny home on wheels (THOW) built on a compliant road trailer is legally a caravan or registered vehicle. It does not need engineered footings. When parked for occupation, it only needs level jack-pads or a temporary pad for the stabilising legs.

A permanent tiny home — fixed to the ground, connected to permanent services, intended for year-round occupation — is legally a Class 1a building. That means a building permit, engineer-certified footings compliant with AS 2870, and inspections. The small footprint does not exempt it.

There is a third path growing in popularity: a relocatable tiny home on skids or a platform that is designed to be lifted onto footings at site, used for years, and potentially re-sited later. For these builds, concrete-free footings are almost always the right answer because they match the relocatable ethos of the structure above.

When you legally need engineered footings

  • Any permanent Class 1a tiny house fixed to land in Victoria.
  • Any tiny home connected to mains power, plumbing or a permanent septic in a way the council treats as a fixed dwelling.
  • Any tiny home being used as a secondary dwelling (for example, a small dependent person's unit) where the council classifies it as a building rather than a movable unit.
  • Most scenarios where you want a long-term insurance policy on the dwelling.

When footings can be much simpler

  • Registered tiny homes on wheels used as a caravan on private land.
  • Short-term stays permitted under council rules (many rural councils allow occupation while building a primary dwelling).
  • Moveable units under the various "tiny home / movable dwelling" policies some councils have introduced — check locally.

Even in these cases, a properly levelled set of footing pads protects the trailer chassis from twist, prevents timber rot, and dramatically extends the life of the home. Many owners still choose a small engineered pad array at the four or six jack-point locations.

The three best footing systems for a tiny home

1. SurePile screw piles — best all-rounder

Screw piles install with a compact drive head, reach below reactive clay, and can be cut to exact height on uneven ground. For a typical 7m x 3m permanent tiny home, six to eight screw piles will do the job, installed in a few hours with virtually no site disturbance. Learn more in our screw pile Melbourne guide.

2. StumpRite — best for stable soils and lighter builds

StumpRite is a driven-stump system that suits stable-to-moderately-reactive soils and lighter loads — precisely the profile of most timber-framed tiny homes. No concrete, no curing, and the stump is ready to build on within minutes of installation.

3. RapidStump — for relocatable or short-life builds

RapidStump suits tiny homes and cabins where speed of install is the priority, and where the dwelling may one day be relocated. The lightweight footprint is ideal for bush sites with difficult vehicle access.

Off-grid and rural Victorian considerations

  • Access — concrete trucks struggle with narrow rural tracks, steep bush driveways and muddy approaches. Concrete-free systems arrive on a ute and trailer.
  • Bushfire overlays — Victoria has wide BAL (Bushfire Attack Level) zones. Steel footings comply better than timber stumps in BAL-12.5 through BAL-FZ.
  • Septic and wastewater — footings must be set out clear of trenches, tanks and absorption fields. Screw piles let you position to millimetre accuracy around existing infrastructure.
  • Slope — a tiny home on a hillside is cheaper to elevate on piles than to pad out with concrete. You also get the subfloor breathing space that protects against moisture.
  • Wildlife and regrowth — no concrete waste, no curing pour, and no spoil heaps. Ideal for Landcare-sensitive blocks.

Typical cost and time ranges

Every site is different, but as a rough guide for a single permanent tiny home in regional Victoria:

  • Soil test — $700 to $1,400 depending on remoteness.
  • Engineering — $1,000 to $2,500.
  • Concrete-free footings installed — typically completed in half a day to one day on site.
  • Equivalent concrete piers — two to three days plus three to four weeks curing before the build starts.

The shorter program means fewer site mobilisation fees, fewer accommodation costs if you're building away from home, and a faster path to lock-up.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Treating a permanent tiny home like a caravan because it feels small.
  • Installing stumps without a soil classification — especially on former farmland where fill may be present.
  • Using timber posts in high-BAL zones.
  • Placing footings before confirming where your septic field will go.
  • Skipping tie-down brackets — a tiny home has a high wind profile for its weight and must resist uplift.
