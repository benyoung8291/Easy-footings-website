Heritage Terrace Restumping in Inner Melbourne: The Complete Guide

By Easy Footings • 17 April 2026 • 8 min read

Inner Melbourne's Victorian-era terraces are beautiful, stubbornly narrow and — increasingly — 140 to 160 years old. That means a lot of them are due for their second or third restump. Whether you're in Carlton, Fitzroy, Collingwood, Richmond, North Melbourne, Parkville or South Melbourne, restumping a heritage terrace is a very different job to restumping a post-war weatherboard in the suburbs. Heritage overlays, party walls, tight side access and lived-in occupation all change the brief. Here is how it's done properly, and why concrete-free systems have quietly become the preferred approach for modern heritage restumping.

What "restumping" really means on a Victorian terrace

A Victorian-era terrace was typically built on timber stumps seated on small concrete pads or even just compacted soil, carrying hardwood bearers and joists beneath the ground-floor timbers. Over 100+ years the original stumps do what timber does underground: they rot at the damp line, slump unevenly, and eventually take the floors and sometimes the internal brick nib walls with them. The symptoms are familiar — sloping floors, cracks over doorways, doors that stick, gaps opening between skirting and floor.

Restumping replaces those aged stumps with a modern engineered footing system, brings the floors back to level (or to a "liveable level" that preserves original features), and locks the subfloor in a stable, ventilated condition for the next century.

Why heritage terraces are a special case

  • Heritage overlays — Most inner-Melbourne suburbs sit under heritage overlays (HO) that restrict changes visible from the street. Restumping is usually exempt where it doesn't alter external fabric, but raising the floor, altering air bricks or lifting veranda columns may trigger a permit.
  • Shared party walls — A terrace shares walls with neighbours on one or both sides. Any restumping work must protect those walls from differential movement. Good restumping plans isolate work zones and jack progressively to avoid transferring load sideways.
  • Tight access — Many terraces have a single side bluestone lane, sometimes 700 mm wide, occasionally no side access at all. Concrete trucks cannot get near, and there's nowhere to stage spoil.
  • Low subfloors — Many inner-Melbourne terraces have crawl spaces of just 400 to 600 mm. Only compact equipment fits, and footings need to be installed with minimal excavation.
  • Delicate plaster and cornices — Decorative lath-and-plaster ceilings, pressed-metal ceilings and original cornices can crack from the tiniest unintended movement. The jacking method matters as much as the footings.
  • Occupied houses — Most owners cannot afford to vacate for a month. Concrete-free restumping lets work continue with occupants in residence for most of the job.

Why concrete-free footings suit heritage terraces

Traditional concrete restumping in a heritage terrace is a logistical nightmare. You need to wheelbarrow concrete through the house or down a narrow lane, dig hand-excavated footing holes in a low subfloor, place reo and formwork, pour, cure for up to 28 days while the house sits on temporary props, and then backfill. The floors often have to stay jacked for weeks.

Concrete-free systems — RapidStump, StumpRite and in some cases SurePile screw piles — change the entire program:

  • No concrete deliveries. No trucks blocking the laneway or the one-way inner-Melbourne street.
  • Compact install equipment that fits crawl spaces and internal access.
  • No curing. The footing is load-ready the moment it's installed, so bearers can be seated and floors released.
  • Clean work area — minimal spoil, minimal mess, very little dust drifting into lived-in rooms.
  • Fully engineered and certified to AS 2870, so insurance and future sale records are clean.

Inner Melbourne suburb-by-suburb notes

  • Carlton and Carlton North — double-fronted and single-fronted Victorians, many over bluestone rubble footings and old clay fill. Concrete-free restumping allows variable embedment depths to handle that variability.
  • Fitzroy and Fitzroy North — heavily heritage-overlayed. Keeping work invisible from the street matters. Subfloor work with compact equipment is the usual approach.
  • Collingwood and Abbotsford — former industrial fill in some pockets, and frequent relocation of services into the subfloor. Screw pile footings can navigate around pipework to millimetre accuracy.
  • Richmond and Cremorne — narrow single-fronted terraces with very restricted side access. The compact RapidStump and StumpRite rigs are often the only equipment that can get to the rear of the block.
  • North Melbourne and Kensington — double-fronted Victorians mixed with two-storey terraces. Party-wall considerations dominate.
  • Parkville and Carlton — many houses on reactive clay with historical subfloor moisture issues; concrete-free stumps combined with improved subfloor ventilation are a common pairing.
  • South Melbourne and Albert Park — lower-lying, occasional fill and high water table pockets. Design considers corrosion protection and higher stump densities.

A typical heritage restump program

  1. Inspection and level survey — every floor is surveyed to establish the existing fall and the target level.
  2. Engineering and permit — a registered building surveyor issues the permit. Heritage advice confirms nothing triggers HO controls.
  3. Set-up and protection — drop sheets, floor protection and dust seals are installed. Furniture is moved progressively, not all at once.
  4. Jacking — rooms are jacked a few at a time using hydraulic bottle jacks on temporary props.
  5. Stump install — old stumps are removed and new engineered stumps or screw piles are installed per the approved design.
  6. Relevelling — floors are lowered back onto the new stumps, brought to the target level and packed off where required.
  7. Finishing — subfloor is cleaned, air bricks are checked and the work area is left tidy.
  8. Certification — the installer issues a statement of compliance and hands over the logs for the title record.

Cost and timeline expectations

Every terrace is different, but as a guide for inner Melbourne:

  • A standard single-fronted two-bedroom Victorian typically carries 18 to 30 stumps.
  • A double-fronted Victorian terrace typically carries 30 to 50 stumps.
  • Concrete-free restump timeline — one to two weeks including jacking, stump install and relevelling.
  • Concrete restump timeline — three to five weeks including curing, with the house sitting on temporary jacks for much longer.
  • Costs vary widely with access, subfloor height and heritage constraints. The premium for concrete-free is usually offset by reduced temporary accommodation, faster return to normal occupation, and cleaner handover.
