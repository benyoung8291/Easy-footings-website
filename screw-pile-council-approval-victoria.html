Do You Need Council Approval for Screw Piles in Victoria?

Understanding building permits, exempt works, and the approval process for screw pile footings across Victoria.

Published 11 April 2026

It is one of the first questions every Victorian homeowner asks before starting a building project: do I need council approval to install screw piles? Whether you are planning a new deck, a granny flat, a shed, or a full house build, understanding the permit requirements upfront can save you time, money, and a lot of frustration.

The short answer is that screw piles themselves do not require a separate approval — they are a footing system, and it is the overall structure that determines whether a building permit is needed. In this guide, we break down when a permit is required, what qualifies as exempt work, and how screw piles can actually make the approval process easier.

When a Building Permit Is Required

In Victoria, the Building Act 1993 and the associated building regulations set out when a building permit is required. As a general rule, you will need a building permit for:

  • New dwellings — any new house, unit, or granny flat requires a full building permit, and the footing design (including screw piles) forms part of the overall structural documentation.
  • Structural alterations and additions — extensions, raised decks attached to the house, and major renovations that affect the building's structure will need a permit.
  • Commercial and industrial projects — virtually all commercial building work requires a permit, regardless of the footing type used.
  • Retaining walls over a certain height — where a retaining wall exceeds the height thresholds set by your local council, a permit is typically needed.

In each of these cases, the screw piles are part of the footing system specified in the engineering plans. The building permit covers the entire structure — foundations included. You do not need a separate permit for the piles themselves, but they must comply with the approved plans and the National Construction Code (NCC).

Exempt Works in Victoria

Not every building project requires a permit. Under the Victorian Building Regulations, certain minor works are classified as exempt — meaning no building permit is needed. Common examples include:

  • Small decks — freestanding decks that are no more than 800 mm above ground level and under 10 square metres may be exempt from a building permit in many circumstances.
  • Garden sheds — non-habitable sheds and outbuildings under 10 square metres (with certain height limits) are often exempt.
  • Some carports — open carports under specific size thresholds may qualify as exempt work, depending on their proximity to boundaries and other buildings.
  • Pergolas and shade structures — lightweight, open-roofed structures may be exempt depending on size and location on the property.

Important: Exemption thresholds vary between councils and can depend on your property's zoning, overlay controls, and bushfire attack level (BAL) rating. A structure that is exempt in one municipality may require a permit in another. Always confirm with your local council before assuming a project is exempt.

Even for exempt works, it is still good practice to use engineered footing products. Using certified screw piles like RapidStump ensures your structure has a sound foundation — whether a permit is required or not.

The Building Permit Process

If your project does require a building permit, here is what the process generally looks like in Victoria:

  1. Engage a registered building surveyor — a private building surveyor or your council's municipal building surveyor will assess your plans and issue the building permit. They are responsible for ensuring your project complies with the NCC and relevant Australian Standards.
  2. Submit plans including footing design — your application must include architectural plans and engineering documentation. The footing design will detail the type of piles (e.g., screw piles), their depth, load capacity, and spacing. If you are using screw piles, the engineer will reference the product's certification and capacity data.
  3. Obtain the building permit — once the surveyor is satisfied that the plans comply, they issue the permit. Work can begin after the permit is issued.
  4. Footing inspection — before the build can continue beyond the footing stage, the building surveyor must inspect the installed footings. For screw piles, this inspection typically confirms that the piles have been installed to the specified depth and torque, and that they match the approved plans.
  5. Continue building — after the footing inspection is passed, construction of the superstructure can proceed. Additional inspections (frame, lockup, final) follow as required.

How Screw Piles Simplify the Process

One of the major advantages of screw piles over traditional concrete footings is how they streamline the building permit and inspection process. Here is why:

  • Engineered products with existing certifications — products like RapidStump, StumpRite, and SurePile come with published load capacity data, CodeMark certification, and engineering documentation. This makes it straightforward for engineers and building surveyors to specify and approve them.
  • Faster inspection turnaround — concrete footings require curing time before they can be inspected and built upon. Screw piles are load-bearing immediately after installation, meaning the footing inspection can happen the same day the piles go in — and construction can continue straight away.
  • Less disruption to the site — screw piles produce no spoil, require no excavation, and leave the site clean. This means fewer complications during the inspection and no need for soil removal or site remediation.
  • Consistent, verifiable installation — each screw pile is installed to a measured torque value, providing clear evidence that it has reached the required bearing capacity. This data is documented and available for the building surveyor at inspection.

In practice, projects using screw piles often move through the approval and inspection stages faster than those using poured concrete, which can save days or even weeks on the overall build timeline. To understand the cost implications, see our screw pile cost guide for Victoria.

Tips for a Smooth Approval

Whether you are a homeowner managing your own project or a builder working across multiple sites, these tips will help the permit process run smoothly:

  • Work with experienced installers — an experienced screw pile installer understands what building surveyors expect at the footing inspection and will document the installation accordingly. Easy Footings provides full installation records for every project.
  • Get a soil report if needed — for new dwellings and larger structures, your engineer will likely require a geotechnical (soil) report. This determines the soil classification and informs the footing design. Screw piles can be specified for a wide range of soil types, including reactive clay.
  • Use certified products — choosing screw pile products with established engineering data and certifications removes uncertainty from the approval process. RapidStump, StumpRite, and SurePile are all widely recognised and accepted by building surveyors throughout Victoria.
  • Communicate with your building surveyor early — involve your building surveyor at the planning stage, not just when you are ready to submit. Early input can prevent costly changes later.
  • Check for overlays and special conditions — heritage overlays, environmental significance overlays, and bushfire-prone areas can all affect what permits you need. Your council's planning department can advise on these.

Important Disclaimer

This article provides general guidance only and is not a substitute for professional advice. Building regulations, exemption thresholds, and council requirements can change and may vary between municipalities. Always check with your local council and a registered building surveyor for the specific requirements that apply to your project and property.
